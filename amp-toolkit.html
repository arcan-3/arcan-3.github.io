The photon budget

Doctorate · Universität Hamburg · 2019–2024 · advisor Prof. Dr. Arp Schnittger · funded by the Human Frontier Science Program

Meiosis was staged by eye. An expert watched a time-lapse, decided which stage each cell was in, and wrote it down. That does not scale, and two experts do not always agree. aMP was an attempt to do it automatically, on imaging that is deliberately worse than the microscope can manage.

The constraint that shapes everything

Live-cell fluorescence imaging has a tension built into it. More illumination gives a better signal-to-noise ratio, and also bleaches the fluorophore and damages the cell you are trying to watch. Image a dividing cell properly and you stop it dividing. So every usable acquisition is deliberately under-illuminated, and everything downstream has to work on data a computer vision benchmark would reject.

Photons are the budget, and the subject sets it. This is the same trade-off as dose reduction in medical imaging.

Why frame-by-frame classification fails

The obvious approach is to segment each frame, classify each cell, stack the results. It does not work, for two reasons that took a while to separate.

The first is identity. Meiocytes look alike, sit in dense clusters, and move between frames — partly because the anther itself drifts. A tracker that drops an identity and picks up its neighbour produces a trajectory blended from two cells, which is worse than no trajectory: it is confidently wrong.

The second is that stage assignments from independent frames produce biologically impossible sequences — a cell that appears to move backwards through the progression, or to skip a stage and return to it. Each individual prediction is plausible. The sequence is nonsense.

What the pipeline does

Four modules, each solving one of those problems, with the outputs of each constrained by what the biology permits.

  • Segmentation and stabilisation. Anther segmentation followed by affine transformation to hold the focal plane steady across frames. Dice 88.9%, IoU 80.8%, F1 88.9% on the held-out test set.
  • Localisation. An ensemble of EfficientDet backbones with non-maximum suppression, evaluated under the COCO protocol: precision 0.77, recall 0.79, F1 0.78.
  • Tracking. A restricted-space motion model that associates detections across frames on normalised cross-correlation and IoU together, rather than searching the whole frame. Cheaper, and less prone to identity switching.
  • Staging. Meiosis phases represented as state vectors, with predicted sequences validated against a knowledge graph so invalid transitions are rejected. Per-stage classification accuracy 76–91%.

What it was for

The pipeline output Z-normalised staging pathways, which compose into a piece-wise timeline of meiotic progression. Applied to tetraploid Arabidopsis thaliana, the timeline showed meiosis I proceeding comparably to diploid while meiosis II is markedly prolonged — and quantified the temporal dynamics of tcx5;6 mutants and ATM insertions, which had not previously been measurable at this resolution without someone watching every video by hand.

The modules were then reused for other quantification tasks in the lab: DNA double-strand-break foci, BiFC analysis, pollen counting. That reuse was the point of building it modularly.

A decision I would defend

Transformer-based segmentation foundation models were becoming available while this work was running. I did not use them. The available hardware could not train or run them at the scale the dataset required, so the work went into CNN backbones and an in-house modular model instead. That was the right call under the constraint, and it is worth stating plainly rather than implying I used whatever was fashionable.
