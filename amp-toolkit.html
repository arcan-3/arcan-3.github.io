Staging cell division from live-cell microscopy

Doctoral work · Universität Hamburg · 2019–2024 · advisor Prof. Dr. Arp Schnittger

Meiosis was staged by eye. An expert watched a time-lapse, decided which stage each cell was in, and wrote it down. That does not scale, and two experts do not always agree. The aMP Toolkit was an attempt to do it automatically, on imaging that is deliberately worse than it could be.

The constraint that shapes everything

Live-cell fluorescence imaging has a tension built into it. More illumination gives you a better signal-to-noise ratio, and also bleaches the fluorophore and damages the cell you are trying to watch. If you image a dividing cell properly, you stop it from dividing. So every usable acquisition is deliberately under-illuminated, and every method downstream has to work on data that a computer vision benchmark would consider unacceptable.

This is the same trade-off as dose reduction in medical imaging. Photons are the budget, and the budget is set by what the subject can survive.

Why frame-by-frame classification fails

The obvious approach is to segment each frame, classify each cell, and stack the results. It does not work, for two reasons that took a while to separate.

The first is identity. Meiocytes look alike, sit in dense clusters, and move between frames. A tracker that loses an identity and picks up its neighbour produces a trajectory that is a blend of two cells, which is worse than no trajectory at all — it is confidently wrong. Object switching was the dominant failure mode throughout.

The second is that stage assignments from independent frames produce sequences that are biologically impossible: a cell that appears to move backwards through the progression, or skip a stage and return to it. Each individual prediction is plausible; the sequence is nonsense.

What the toolkit does

  • Domain-adapted segmentation with stabilisation. A segmentation network adapted to the imaging conditions, coupled with video stabilisation and multi-view reconstruction so that a cell keeps its identity across frames.
  • 3D position and pose estimation. Cells are localised and oriented in the volume rather than in a single projection, which makes tracking robust when the viewpoint or the cell's orientation changes.
  • State-based temporal modelling. Stage assignment is constrained to valid transitions rather than being decided per frame. This is the part that turned a noisy classifier into something an expert would accept.

Adapting a foundation model where labels are scarce

Alongside the staging work, I fine-tuned the Segment Anything Model for a set of plant-science segmentation tasks — root length, leaf chlorosis, BiFC analysis, foci quantification. These have almost no labelled data and imaging characteristics far from anything in SAM's pretraining distribution. The interesting result was how little supervision it took to make it useful, which matters for any domain where annotation requires an expert rather than a crowd worker.
