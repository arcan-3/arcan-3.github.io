Four years underwater

Bremen UAS · smartPORT, TUHH · Helmut Schmidt Universität · 2014–2018

On a CV these are three entries at three institutions. They were one subject approached from three directions: work out what the channel does to a signal, transmit through it anyway, then pull a specific source back out of it.

The medium is the difficulty

A water column is a hostile channel. Sound travels slowly enough that platform motion produces significant Doppler. Sound speed varies with depth, temperature and salinity, so rays bend. Surface and seabed reflect, so every transmission arrives several times at different delays and amplitudes. Ambient noise is non-stationary and often biological — the shipping traffic and the animals are both in your band. Almost everything that makes RF communication tractable is absent.

2014–15 — characterising the channel

A project on channel state estimation in a water column at Bremen University of Applied Sciences, under Prof. Dr. Dieter Kraus. The underlying question: given what arrives at the receiver, what can be said about the path it took? Estimate the channel and the rest becomes tractable. Fail to, and nothing downstream is reliable.

2016–17 — transmitting through it

Chirp Modulation for Resilient Underwater Acoustic Communication · smartPORT, TUHH · supervised by Prof. Dr. Bernd-Christian Renner

smartPORT operates micro autonomous underwater vehicles — small, cheap, low-powered, used for things like pollution and oil-spill detection, port traffic monitoring and hull inspection. Several of them have to synchronise before they can talk to each other, using packet-based preamble detection. The group was using frequency shift keying, and multipath was smearing the preamble boundary badly enough that detection was ambiguous.

Chirp modulation spreads a symbol across a band of frequencies over time instead of sitting on one carrier. Correlate a chirp against its template and you get a sharp peak. Correlate FSK and you get a triangular region — which multipath distorts until no clear maximum survives. That difference is the entire argument, and it is visible in a single plot.

I built the signal generation algorithm and the evaluation chain, characterised the acoustic modem and the Tiepie Handyscope instrumentation, and worked out a parameter set that held up. Then tested it across four environments of increasing realism: a small container, a test tank, Channel Harburg, and Außenmühle Lake. Simulation would have been easier and would have proved less.

Preamble detection delay dropped from seconds to milliseconds against FSK, across ten iterations, with robustness to both multipath and Doppler shift.

Two secondary results mattered more than they look. The time–bandwidth product turned out proportional to power concentration in the main lobe, which is what let me choose parameters deliberately rather than by sweep. And generation sampling could be reduced from 2 MHz to 500 kHz before spurious correlation peaks appeared around the true detection point — the number that decides whether any of this ports to a Cortex M4 on a battery.

2017–18 — pulling a source back out of it

Source Separation and Echo Detection of Underwater Acoustic Signals of Whales · Helmut Schmidt Universität · supervisor Carlos de Obaldía, M.Sc. · examiners Prof. Dr.-Ing. habil. Udo Zölzer and Prof. Dr. Bernd-Christian Renner

Passive acoustic monitoring: no transmitter, only what arrives at the hydrophone. The recordings contain several species at once, plus the ship the array is attached to. The goal was to detect echolocation clicks of toothed whales and extract enough from each click to identify the species.

Separation first — isolating clicks from whistles, creaks and other harmonic vocalisations using generalised Wiener filtering, with non-negative matrix factorization, median filtering and kernel additive modelling compared against each other by peak-to-noise ratio and signal-to-distortion ratio.

Then three features from the separated clicks, because echo detection in the usual sense is not available when you do not know where the source is:

  • Echo decay time, via Hilbert transform analysis — which established that each click pulse carries superimposed pulses inside it.
  • A high- and low-energy split of each pulse, using continuous wavelet analysis to find those inlying short-term pulses and the frequency bands they occupy.
  • Click length, through a correlation technique adapted to exploit the band limitation of each pulse.

The first feature is what made the other two possible. Finding structure inside what looked like a single pulse changed the problem from measuring a click to decomposing one.

Where it went next

In 2025 I worked on Wi-Fi Channel State Information for passive environmental sensing — estimating an RF channel and reading motion and presence out of how it changes. It is the Bremen problem in a different medium, eleven years later. The propagation physics differs. The estimation problem does not.
